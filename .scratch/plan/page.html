A plan for Poppies · November 2026

The balloon studio that lives on the coast.

This is one plan for the whole business: what Poppies sells, what it charges, how the first year goes, and what it earns. It's built from twelve rounds of market research and 172 ideas. The research laid out options. This picks.

Every pick is a recommendation, and you can overrule any of them. Twelve of them are yours alone to make. They're at the end, and you can answer them right on this page.

Poppies is the balloon installation studio that lives on the central Oregon coast: built with air, nothing released, everything collected, and booked in a week.

Why it can work

Five things the research found.

  1. Nobody local does it. Every installation rival drives in from Salem or Portland, 1½ to 2½ hours away. The nearest asks for two weeks' notice. The one local balloon shop sells gift bouquets.

  2. The money is visitors. Lincoln County has about 51,000 residents and about $600 million a year in visitor spending. People come to the coast to celebrate.

  3. Nobody publishes a price. Not one coast vendor. Price is the first thing a couple filters on, so the first studio to say "from $500" gets the enquiry.

  4. The coast has few venues, and you have a tent. Coast weddings happen at resorts, on lawns and at rental houses. A 20×40 tent with the decor and the permit handled is a venue you can bring with you.

  5. Balloons are cheap. Your time is the product. Materials are under a tenth of a garland's price. So the whole plan is built around what an hour of your time earns.

The order of things

Three engines, started one at a time.

The 172 ideas sort into three lines of work. They start at different speeds, so the plan runs them in sequence.

Celebrations

Starts first · from January

The Arrival, The Tideline, The Headland and The Question, sold to visitors and locals. The channel is property managers, who already email every guest before they arrive. Short lead times, work all year, and every job is a photograph and a review.

Year-one goal: 30 Arrivals and 36 garlands.

Standing work

Carries winter · sold in August

The Standing Order, holiday lobbies and windows, ribbon cuttings. Sold to coast shops, inns, hotels and the casino. It repeats, it fills October to March, and one route day covers several shops.

Year-one goal: four shops on order by autumn, and two holiday lobbies.

Weddings and The Pavilion

Pays most · planted now, harvested next year

Couples book 6 to 12 months ahead and want proof. So year one plants: a styled shoot in March, a kit for venues, photographers as partners, and a gathering of coast wedding professionals in the tent. The Pavilion earns about twice what anything else does per hour.

Year-one goal: three Pavilions, five weddings, and next summer half booked by December.

Your sculpture runs through all three. It's the one thing no rival can copy. One public piece each season, at a kite festival, the aquarium or a library, keeps Poppies in front of the whole coast for the price of a day's work.

What could go wrong

Six risks, and what the plan does about each.

The name. POPPIE is reportedly a registered trademark for balloon design.Check it this week, before the domain, signs or filings. A lawyer's hour if it's live. The website holds the name in one setting, so a change takes minutes.
Wind and salt air.Air-filled, weighted designs. A stated wind speed above which a piece moves under cover. The tent as the fallback.
Too much work for one person.No more than three installs a week, a minimum price, and prices that rise before hours do.
A quiet winter.The standing-work engine exists for this. It's sold in August.
A balloon ban or backlash.Nothing released, nothing called "biodegradable", everything collected. Oregon nearly banned releases in 2025. Poppies is already on the right side of it.
The research is wrong somewhere.It was done from search results, not site visits. Local facts need a phone call before money rides on them. The careful version assumes the property managers are slow.
