# The Poppies plan

2026-11-11. One plan, built from everything in `.scratch/`: twelve research
streams, 172 ideas, the pricing proposal and the break-even model. The earlier
work laid out options. **This document picks.** Every pick is a recommendation
for the owner to overrule, and the twelve calls that are hers alone are in
`decisions-for-her.md`.

Two cautions apply throughout. The research rests on search snippets, so the
facts marked *check* need a phone call before money rides on them. And the
numbers are scenarios, not forecasts.

## The business in one sentence

> **Poppies is the balloon installation studio that lives on the central
> Oregon coast: built with air, nothing released, everything collected, and
> booked in a week.**

## Why this can work

1. **Nobody local does it.** Every installation rival drives in from Salem or
   Portland, 1.5 to 2.5 hours away. The nearest asks for two weeks' notice.
   The one local balloon shop sells gift bouquets (*check* that it's open).
2. **The money is visitors, not residents.** Lincoln County has about 51,000
   residents and about $600M a year in visitor spending. People come to the
   coast to celebrate: birthdays in rental houses, proposals on headlands,
   weddings on lawns.
3. **Nobody publishes a price.** Not one coast vendor. Price is the first
   thing a couple filters on, so the first studio to say "from $500" gets the
   enquiry.
4. **The coast has no venues, and she has a tent.** Coast weddings happen at
   resorts or on lawns and at rental houses. A 20×40 tent plus the decor,
   with the permit handled, is a venue she can bring with her.
5. **Balloons are cheap. Her time is the product.** Materials are under 10%
   of a garland's price. So the plan is built around what an hour of her
   time earns, not around selling more balloons.

travel by zone along Highway 101, teardown as its own line ($120 local), a
50% deposit, and helium only as an add-on.

## Three engines, in order

The 172 ideas sort into three lines of business. They start at different
speeds, so the plan runs them in sequence.

### 1. Celebrations: the engine that starts first

The Arrival, The Tideline, The Headland, The Question. Sold to visitors and
locals, mostly through **property managers** who already email every guest
before they arrive.

- **Why first:** short lead times, year-round demand, and no portfolio of
  weddings needed. Each job is a photo and a review.
- **The channel:** three to five property managers offering The Arrival in
  their pre-arrival emails, for a 10–15% referral fee. Nobody on the coast
  sells this today (*check*).
- **Year-one goal:** 30 Arrivals and 36 garlands.

### 2. Standing work: the engine that carries winter

The Standing Order, holiday lobbies and windows, ribbon cuttings, real estate
kits. Sold to coast businesses.

- **Why second:** it repeats, it fills October to March, and it's sold in
  late summer while weddings are busiest. One route day covers several shops.
- **The channel:** walk into shops with a photo of their own window mocked
  up. Chambers for ribbon cuttings. Hotels and the casino for holiday
  lobbies, pitched in August and September.
- **Year-one goal:** four shops on a Standing Order by autumn, and two
  holiday lobbies.

### 3. Weddings and The Pavilion: the engine that pays most

Wedding installations and the tent. The Pavilion earns about twice what
anything else does per hour.

- **Why third:** couples book 6 to 12 months ahead and want proof. Year one
  plants: a styled shoot in March, a venue kit, photographers as partners,
  and a mixer for coast wedding professionals in the tent.
- **Year-one goal:** three Pavilions, five weddings, and next summer's
  calendar half full by December.

**The art runs through all three.** Her figure sculpture is the thing no
rival can copy. One public piece each season (a kite festival, the aquarium,
a library) keeps Poppies in front of the whole coast for the price of a day's
work.

## The money

Three scenarios, from `numbers.py`. Each uses the menu prices above, $4,000 of
yearly fixed costs, and 27.5% of profit set aside for tax.

| | Careful | Expected | Strong |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Jobs in the year | 53 | 100 | 165 |
| Monthly shop visits | 12 | 36 | 72 |
| Revenue | $32,400 | $67,900 | $118,100 |
| Take-home after tax set-aside | $15,100 | $34,900 | $62,600 |
| Hours a week, admin included | 14 | 23 | 35 |

- **Careful** is one job a week. It's a realistic first year started from
  nothing, and it already pays for itself: about **a dozen jobs cover the
  year's fixed costs**.
- **Expected** is two jobs a week plus four shops. It's the year-one target
  and the likely year two.
- **Strong** is three jobs a week plus six shops. That's the ceiling for one
  person, and the point to bring in a helper.

**To start:** about $1,750 lean, or $5,600 comfortable, not counting a
vehicle. The first four jobs pay back the lean version.

## How it's run

`job-playbook.md` has the full routine. The rules that matter most:

- **Reply within 24 hours, "no" included.** Rivals ghost people.
- **Two checks on every quote:** the price is at least 4× materials plus
  mileage, and it pays her $45 an hour or more with the driving counted.
- **No more than three installs a week** while she works alone.
- **Photograph every piece before guests arrive.** The archive is the
  marketing.
- **Leave nothing behind.** A fragment sweep at teardown, with a photo to
  the client.
- **Ask every client for a review, the same way every time,** and never
  reward one.

## The five numbers to watch

Looked at every Monday. All five fit on a card on the fridge.

1. Enquiries this week, and where each came from.
2. Hours from enquiry to her reply.
3. Jobs booked, and the average price.
4. Dollars per hour on each finished job.
5. Google reviews, in total.

## What could go wrong

| Risk | What the plan does about it |
| --- | --- |
| **The name.** POPPIE is reportedly a registered trademark for balloon design | Check it this week, before the domain, signs or filings. A lawyer's hour if it's live. The site holds the name in one setting, so a change costs minutes |
| **Wind and salt air** | Air-filled, weighted designs. A published "we move indoors above this wind speed" rule. The tent as the fallback |
| **Too much work for one person** | The three-a-week cap, the minimum, and prices that rise before hours do. A helper at the Strong scenario |
| **A quiet winter** | The standing-work engine exists for this. It's sold in August |
| **A balloon ban or backlash** | Nothing released, nothing called "biodegradable", everything collected. Oregon nearly banned releases in 2025; Poppies is already on the right side of it |
| **The research is wrong somewhere** | Every *check* in these files is a phone call. The careful scenario assumes the property-manager channel is slow |

## Years two and three

- **Year two:** weddings and The Pavilion become the largest line. A weekend
  helper. The wedding-pro mixer becomes an annual date on the coast's
  calendar. A second tent look for winter.
- **Year three:** a named seasonal collection each spring. A gallery show in
  a vacant storefront. Classes in the quiet months. Poppies is the name
  planners in Portland give when a client says "the coast".
