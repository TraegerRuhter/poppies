# How comparable businesses package their offerings

Type: research
Status: resolved

## Question

How do balloon and small event-decor businesses structure their service menus,
and which structures hold up as the business grows?

Look for the *shape*, not the prices:

- **À la carte versus tiered packages versus quote-only.** Which do established
  balloon businesses actually use, and is there a pattern by size or by market?
- **How businesses that started in parties and moved into weddings restructured**
  — did they add a tier, split the brand, or keep one menu? This is the closest
  analogue to Poppies' intended path and the most valuable thing to find.
- **How a "starting at $X" price anchor is used** on sites that do not publish
  full price lists, and whether it appears to filter enquiries or deter them.
- **How add-on services get presented** without making a menu feel like a
  checkout.
- **Named-package conventions** — whether packages are named by scale (Petite /
  Grand), by occasion (Birthday / Baby Shower), or by component count, and which
  reads as most credible for a business with a small portfolio.

## Why this blocks

The packaging shape is the "rule for how a new offering gets added" that the
destination asks for. Choosing it blind risks inventing a structure that no
customer recognises; the point of this ticket is to find the conventions worth
adopting and the ones worth deliberately breaking.

## Answer

Full findings: `../research/02-packaging-patterns.md`. Same proxy limitation as
ticket 01 — snippet-based, with per-claim confidence marked in the file.

**Recommended shape: two tracks divided by a stated minimum, with figure work
outside both.**

- **Track A — a short published menu.** Three or four line items, priced per foot
  or per piece with a "from" figure, and a minimum that makes small jobs viable.
- **Track B — one quote-led offering** for weddings and larger events, with a
  single stated minimum and **no tiers at all**. One door, one number, then a
  conversation.
- **Sculptural figure work sits apart from both.** It does not price per linear
  foot, and folding it into a garland menu would both mis-price it and hide the
  thing that differentiates her.

**Why not tiers.** Tiered packages are a promise of *repeatability* — "I have
built this exact thing often enough to know what it costs." A small portfolio
cannot evidence that, and a tier with no photograph of that tier reads as
aspirational. A component menu makes a smaller, truer claim, and every item on it
can be backed by a photograph she actually has.

**Why not quote-only.** It is the premium posture, but it is one that costs money
to hold — it works when portfolio and referral network do the qualifying a price
would otherwise do. She has neither yet.

**The headline finding on growth: no example was found of a balloon business
splitting into a second brand for weddings.** Searched for specifically and
repeatedly. Absence of evidence through a snippet-only channel is weak, but the
positive pattern is strong and consistent: **one brand, occasion-segmented pages,
and the restructuring happens somewhere other than the menu.**

Three patterns underneath that:

- **Occasion pages are navigation, not packaging** (highest confidence in the
  ticket — it rests on URL paths rather than snippets). Eight businesses build a
  landing page per occasion off a single brand. The garland is the same garland.
  **This directly validates the website's Celebrations/Weddings doorway: the split
  survives as navigation and tone, and does not need to become an offering
  split.**
- **Where these businesses genuinely bifurcate, the line is buy-it-now versus
  commission-it** — not birthdays versus weddings. Growing into weddings does not
  need a wedding menu; it needs a commissioned track that did not exist at $200
  birthday garlands.
- **Parties get packages, weddings get consultations.** Searching for named
  wedding balloon tiers returned nothing, while the same search for birthdays
  returns occasion-named packages immediately. The restructuring shows up as a
  different call to action, not a new tier.

**The mature version of this structure comes from wedding florists**, who have
been solving the same problem for decades: an à la carte line with a low or no
minimum, and full-service work behind a stated minimum. **The minimum — not the
brand, not the tier — is what lets one business serve a $300 birthday and a
$3,000 wedding without cheapening either.**

**Bears on ticket 09:** the evidence points hard against splitting the brand.
Every growth example kept one name. Splitting would double the portfolio problem —
two brands each with half the evidence.

**Bears on ticket 08:** the real gate on wedding work is credibility
infrastructure, not menu design — venue preferred-vendor lists and styled shoots.
Preferred-vendor lists are precisely where insurance requirements bite, which
connects to the risk already flagged as out of scope on the map.
